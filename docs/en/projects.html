Project overview

This project explores a dataset on AI usage and student life. The objective is to assess whether AI usage is associated with academic performance, career confidence, ethical concerns, and perceived productivity gains.

The analysis was conducted in R, using mainly the tidyverse, base statistical functions, and a limited number of additional libraries for modeling and result formatting (like MASS for ordered / multinomial models, broom for tidy regression models, and stats for clustering).

The project is exploratory. Since the dataset does not include a control group – a distinct group of students not using AI – the results cannot unwind causal relationships. The objective is not to prove that AI improves student outcomes, but to evaluate whether the available data supports meaningful statistical relationships.

Analysis

Analytical questions and main variables

The analysis focuses on five main questions:

  1. Is there a measurable change in GPA after AI usage?
  2. Do AI usage variables explain GPA change?
  3. Is career confidence associated with GPA or AI usage?
  4. Are ethical concerns structured by AI usage behavior?
  5. Can students be segmented into meaningful AI usage profiles?

GPA scores, career confidence, and perceived ethical concerns were selected as the main dependent variables. These indicators are commonly used in educational and training-related research, and provide a standardized framework for assessing potential outcomes associated with AI usage.

The dataset includes only two socio-demographic variables, Age and Major. This limited set of control variables restricts the ability to account for structural heterogeneity across students (e.g., socio-economic background, prior academic trajectory, institutional context), and therefore constrains the depth of any impact analysis.

In addition, the AI usage variables (task frequency, main usage case, primary tool, and time saved) are self-reported measures collected through a questionnaire. As such, they reflect subjective perceptions rather than observed behavior, which introduces potential biases (recall bias, social desirability bias, and approximation effects).

Finally, descriptive statistics indicate a high level of uniformity in distributions across the 1,500 observations. Combined with the discretized structure of several variables (notably GPA), this raises questions regarding the empirical richness of the dataset. The data may be simplified, simulated, or heavily standardized, which limits variability and reduces the likelihood of identifying strong statistical relationships.

WarningMethodological positioning

As a result, the analysis should be interpreted as an exploratory exercise focused on methodological rigor rather than as evidence of real-world relationships.

The distribution is discretized, most likely due to rounding effects in GPA values: a GPA score in the present dataset is systematically rounded up.

This limits strict normality, but the sample size (=1500) is large enough for the paired t-test to remain robust. A non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test is also used as a robustness check in Section 1.3.3.3.

GPA statistical testing

The first inferential question is deliberately simple: do students report a higher GPA after AI usage than before?

TipStatistical test: paired t-test

Research question: Is post-AI GPA significantly different from baseline GPA?

Null hypothesis (H0): There is no mean difference between baseline GPA and post-AI GPA.

Alternative hypothesis (H1): There is a mean difference between baseline GPA and post-AI GPA.

Method: A paired t-test is used because both GPA measures are observed for the same students.

Assumptions: The key normality assumption applies to the paired differences. As shown in Section 1.3.3.2, the difference is discretized and not perfectly normal. However, given the large sample size, the paired t-test should remain robust.

t_test <- t.test(

TipRobustness check: Wilcoxon signed-rank test

Alternative hypothesis (H1): The median paired difference is not equal to zero.

Method: The Wilcoxon signed-rank test ranks the absolute paired differences and checks whether positive and negative ranks are balanced.

Reason for use: This test is less sensitive to deviations from normality and is appropriate as a robustness check after the diagnostic findings in Section 1.3.3.2.

Our next step is to test whether AI usage variables actually explain this change.

Modeling GPA outcomes

The statistical tests show that GPA increases on average. The modeling stage asks a different question: can AI usage variables explain variation in GPA outcomes?

Naive model: GPA difference explained by AI usage
TipModel 1: OLS model on GPA difference

Research question: Do AI usage variables explain GPA change?

Dependent variable: gpa_diff = GPA_Post_AI - GPA_Baseline

Null hypothesis (H0): AI usage variables have no association with GPA change.

Alternative hypothesis (H1): At least one AI usage variable is associated with GPA change.

Method: OLS regression is used as a first explanatory model.

Assumptions and diagnostics: Residual diagnostics are inspected after model estimation. As discussed in Section 1.3.3.2, the dependent variable is discretized, so the model is treated as exploratory rather than definitive in terms of impact analysis.

In order to solve any issues related to the form of the dependent variable (GPA_Post_AI - GPA_Baseline), and to futher control interpersonal variations, we decided to use in the next model the original GPA scores, with GPA_Post_AI being the new dependent variable.

Adjusted model: post-AI GPA controlled by baseline GPA
ImportantWhy baseline GPA must be controlled

The previous model ignores the starting academic level of each student. This is problematic because GPA is bounded: students with a high baseline GPA have less room to improve.

To account for this ceiling effect, post-AI GPA is modeled while controlling for baseline GPA.

Main results

The analysis produces several consistent findings across descriptive statistics, hypothesis testing, modeling, and clustering.

1. Strong persistence of academic performance

Post-AI GPA is almost entirely determined by baseline GPA. Across all models, prior performance explains the overwhelming majority of variance, indicating a highly stable academic structure.

2. No measurable effect of AI usage variables

AI-related variables — tool, usage frequency, usage case, and time saved — do not meaningfully explain academic outcomes.

This result is robust across:

  • OLS models
  • logistic models
  • correlation analysis

AI usage intensity does not translate into measurable GPA differences.

3. Negative association between time saved and performance

Time saved is the only AI-related variable showing a statistically significant effect in the final model, with a small negative coefficient.

This suggests that perceived efficiency gains may not correspond to improved learning outcomes.

4. Weak structure of subjective variables

Career confidence and ethical concerns are only weakly connected to both GPA and AI usage variables.

These dimensions appear largely independent from observed academic performance.

Ethical concern shows only a weak association with AI usage frequency and an effect of familiarity: the more you use the AI tools, the less you are concerned with their ethical implications.

5. Existence of latent performance profiles

Clustering reveals three distinct profiles primarily structured by:

  • baseline academic level
  • GPA evolution
  • Career confidence score

AI usage variables do not strongly differentiate these groups.

When integrated into regression models, clusters remain statistically very significant, indicating the presence of unobserved heterogeneity in academic trajectories.

6. AI as a capability-dependent amplifier

The combined evidence suggests that AI does not function as a universal performance enhancer.

Instead:

AI appears to act as a capability-dependent amplifier, where outcomes depend on baseline skills and the ability to use the tool effectively.

  • intermediate students benefit the most;
  • high performers show limited gains (ceiling effect);
  • lower-performing students may not benefit, or may even be negatively affected.

Interpretation

The dataset does not support a strong relationship between AI usage and academic outcomes. The strongest statistical relationship found here is the continuity between baseline GPA and post-AI GPA.

This suggests that academic performance is highly stable, while AI usage, career confidence, ethical concern, and time saved are weakly connected in this dataset.

In addition to self-reporter measurement biases and a probability of artificial origin of the dataset, this observation makes us believe that the relationship between the AI technologies and GPA scores is probably moderated by other variables not recorded in the collected data.

Cluster analysis yielded the most advanced results and gives us a strong impulse to suggest that AI effectiveness depends on the interaction between baseline capability and tool usage not recorded in the dataset variables.

AI is not a primary driver of performance. AI acts as a capability amplifier or destabilizer, depending on the user profile. The observed clusters are emergent outcomes of this interaction.

The relationship between baseline academic ability and AI gains appears non-monotonic, suggesting an inverted-U type dynamic.

  • For high-performing students, AI operates under a ceiling constraint. Gains are mechanically limited, and usage appears strategic rather than transformative.
  • Students with intermediate baseline performance exhibit the highest marginal returns. Their skill set appears sufficiently developed to leverage AI outputs critically, while still leaving substantial room for improvement.
  • For lower-performing students, AI usage may substitute rather than augment learning processes. Limited evaluative capacity can lead to inefficient or counterproductive use, resulting in negligible or even negative outcomes.

The results suggest that the effectiveness of AI is mediated not by usage intensity, but by usage quality — i.e., the ability to critically interpret, adapt, and integrate AI-generated outputs.

Observed variables capture intensity of use, but not cognitive engagement. This likely explains the absence of strong statistical relationships.

Our interpretations remain hypothetical, as the dataset does not include direct measures of cognitive strategies, AI literacy, or study behaviors.

ImportantAI literacy and usage quality

AI does not appear to equalize academic performance. Instead, it reinforces existing trajectories: it marginally benefits high performers, significantly supports intermediate students, and may disadvantage those lacking the skills required to use it effectively.

The observed pattern is consistent with a skill-biased effect of AI usage, where returns depend on the user’s ability to integrate the tool into existing cognitive processes. Matthew effect: “the rich get richer”.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered:

  • The dataset is observational and does not include a control group.
  • GPA differences are discretized and affected by rounding.
  • AI usage variables are self-reported rather than directly observed.
  • The dataset includes only limited socio-demographic controls.
  • Some variables appear weakly connected, suggesting limited internal structure.
  • Statistical significance does not necessarily imply practical impact.
  • The dataset may be synthetic or simplified, which limits real-world generalization.
